# Principal-AI-agent problems

Principal-agent problems show up everywhere in management, economics and finance. The setup is extremely general: A principal has a task that he or she wants done and decides to delegate this task to an agent to carry it out, but incentives may not align. It could be a boss and subordinate, a teacher and student, or a wealthy individual endowing a financial manager with his wealth, with the goal of preserving or growing it. We live in a media environment that is absolutely flooded with the concept of agents, specifically LLM-based ones with access to a computer. As the last example above shows, there is really no restriction on the biology of principal or agent, indeed a financial manager in this sense is usually a company, with many managers, employees, shareholders and operating rules (with their own principal-agent relationships), constituting together an organization. So one can readily start asking questions about the relationship between human and AI agent. 

In many of the examples above, the objective of the principal is clear, and the principles underlying actions of the agent are sufficiently well understood to design mechanisms that will elicit the right actions from the agent. The teacher has a mandate to increase the knowledge and skills of his students, which he or she will in turn have to reliably show the rector of the school in some way. The student probably has some interest in learning the subject, but of course this can vary a lot, and in mandatory schooling there are probably many other things more tempting. A test at the end of the semester is a strong incentive to learn, but depending on time-preference, attention and other such factors it might make sense to design schooling around more continuous in-class assessment, as is in general done up through middle and even high school, only to be drastically reduced at university, where the self-motivation and long-term planning can be expected to have increased or matured. Even in a very simple model incorporating some of the factors we have discussed here in words, one can see how the general shape of education from pre-school up to university is shaped by trying to come up with reasonable or even optimal mechanisms in light of how the principal-agent problem, and more importantly the characteristics of the agent, change over the course of time. 

There are also situations where an optimal contract or mechanism is very hard to come up with, at least in a way that is possible to formally model. In fact, the first example of a boss and his or her subordinate fits this pattern quite well. Coase proposed explaining the boundary of the firm based on how difficult contracting is in this setting. If and when it is possible to specify exactly what task a subordinate should do, it's more likely to be outsourced, subject to data security and coordination considerations. Many of the tasks that are outsourced are more manual, repetitive or easy to specify... they are the opposite of messy, context-dependent and judgement-oriented roles that are among those closest to the core of the business. 

I think this example is worth keeping in mind when we think about LLM-based AI agents. Here are some questions, in no particular order: 
- What is it that we want AI agents like Muse (Meta) or Dots (OpenAI) to do for us? 
- When they take autonomous actions on our behalf, who are they really doing it for and can we get any guarantees that it is with our best interests in mind?
- How are our 'best interests' determined and represented by such an agent? 

What do we want them to do for us? A use case that is often brought up, probably because it has become canonical and somewhat of a benchmark, is that of travel bookings. There are other examples that are very similar in spirit, in that they promise an increase in convenience, like buying groceries, booking restaurants or scheduling calendar activities. Then there are also some examples that promise better finances, either by cancelling unused subscriptions, reconciling card payments or straight up managing finances, moving unused cash into low risk ETFs or bonds... and somewhat dramatically there was this extremely short note by Apollo chief economist on how this might cause future 'agentic bank runs'. 

I think these two groups of examples are very different in their utility. The first convenience-increasing group I'm very sceptical of, since it's often the case that we want to enjoy the tingling feeling of browsing, weighing different options and then making a decision. There is probably no shortage of consumption choices that felt better while booked or bought than during consumption, and perhaps one never has to actually consume. There is this strange South Korean phenomenon of so-called "dopamine sites". People scroll for options, hit buy with fake money and then have nothing whatsoever delivered to them "FoodNeverArrives"; a situation where only this nominally inconvenient part of shopping is the product. There is an enjoyment of the process of shopping itself, and without having any sources for this claim, I feel it safe to argue that as societies get richer and people have more leisure time, this component of the shopping experience increases in importance. 

The second group of use cases seems much more useful and promising. If I can track down and even recover some of what I'm spending on my AI subscriptions by cancelling others that I don't use, that's a clearcut improvement. If invoices, reconciliation and personal finance can be checked and taken care of, the time gains could be significant, and even more so the removal of constant background stress that arises whenever one is swamped with a host of small tasks that nevertheless require some amount of activation energy to be handled. Managing finances to the extent of overseeing investment is trickier. It brings algorithmic trading and the like to mind, but that's rather unlikely as a use case. The latency and sophistication required for that is not going to reside in a retail AI agent, especially not as big hedge and quant funds are continuing to invest in supercomputing setups. But retail finance is heavily under-optimized at this stage: Only wealthy people have wealth managers. There are a lot of allocation changes that can be made to a typical portfolio, that yield improvements under many reasonable assumptions. A large proportion of European households store most of their savings in deposits for example, and while the reasons for this are certainly both institutional and behavioural, having a system one can trust providing nudges in the right direction, or even just doing things for you, moving those funds to a low-risk ETF for example, could be quite helpful. 

So should we 'trust' such AI agents? In all of the above examples, except for the most clearcut ones, the right decision depends crucially on our own subjective preferences. This is not really an issue with faulty invoices or similar, but to manage and move money around there needs to be an adequate mapping of my personal risk preference. In personal shopping, the agent needs some notion of what I would like to buy or what deal would induce me to get it now rather than later. The temptation will be to work from the other direction: What action can I take to make the consumer (principal in this case) happy? How can I increase the metric of dollars spent through the agent? This is the question such an agent company will ask, because it's the easiest to measure and most aligned with their financial goals. Unfortunately, it risks bringing out some of the same misalignment as current-day social media feeds, dominated by short-form content and screen addiction, with documented negative health effects. This is exacerbated by the fact that the principal-agent problem of this setting is challenging in a number of ways: Preferences change over time and can be manipulated. Since they are not static, it's extremely difficult to measure. 

When a language model is tasked with making simple investment decisions, it'll probably have an implied risk-preference that is quite different from its human. I ran a small-scale experiment based on the experimental setup in “Improving Rationality by Increasing Attention” (Netzer et al., 2025), giving Codex a sequence of lottery choices, multiple rounds with 11 options, all on the same budget line, and asking it to select the best one. Without getting too technical, there are ways to measure to what extent the choices made across these rounds abide by certain natural axioms of rationality. I had expected smaller models to violate these axioms more often, and that there would be some predictable decline with increasing model size. Instead, during the first round of the experiment, all the models I tested showed zero rationality deviations on the measures I tested. When examining the choices more carefully it became clear why: The models chose to maximize the expected gain every time; they were risk-neutral. Since the lottery setup is very basic, they had no problem performing this simple calculation, or even one-shotting it every single time. I then appended the embarrassingly simple "Don't be risk-neutral" to the end of the prompt, and promptly started seeing rationality violations in the smaller model. With the largest model, Astra, it specified the utility function it would be using for all rounds, and then stuck to that consistently, showing no deviations in rationality. The takeaway here (preliminary results and some hypotheses requiring further and better experiments) is that 1. this should probably be examined in more complicated setups, to see more violations and how they vary; 2. the models seem to default to utility functions that are theoretically well-motivated, but not necessarily the ones a human would abide by; 3. bigger models, reasoning and test-time compute make decisions better, probably both their rational and subjective components, and it would be interesting to push the limits on the latter, i.e. to see how well subjective actions align with a principal as test time compute is scaled up. 

So far this lies completely at the output level, in the sense that we just observe what the model or agent does and then record that. One slight subtlety which blurs the lines somewhat is that I did peek into the thinking trace summary that Codex provides, i.e. in my retelling above I mention how Astra determined ahead of time what utility function it would be using, and I could then see that the choices it made aligned with that. (But I'd argue that this is still very much part of the model output) In the Netzer study, they administer concentration-enhancing drugs and use eye-tracking software to probe where subjects direct their attention under the influence. They find that this varies by drug, and that compared to placebo, it helps direct attention towards 'the right part of the budget line' and then at the output level one consequently sees a decrease in rationality violations. This is going one step further than simply observing behaviour. Can something similar be done in my small experiment, perhaps with an open-source model? Can I peek inside and see what is going on in how the model processes the decision (akin to the eye tracking, perhaps monitoring the chain of thought, or even at the level of computation)? Would it even be possible to perturb it mid-decision by altering some internal state representation, say by adding a vector at some level of computation, analogous to administering a drug? Perhaps not necessarily a drug for concentration, but maybe one for risk-taking? These questions seem more like mechanistic interpretability. 

By looking inside, can I get some guarantees that it does things in my best interests?
